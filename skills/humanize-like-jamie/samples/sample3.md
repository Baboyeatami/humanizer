(Abstract) —This paper presents an accuracy-latency benchmark of YOLOv8n and RF-DETR Nano for multiclass sugarcane leaf disease detection using a Philippine sugarcane leaf disease dataset export. The benchmark covers four economically relevant disease classes: brown rust, leaf scald, red rot, and smut. The prepared model-ready export contained 2,720 training images, 173 validation images, and 171 held-out test images resized to 640 x 640 pixels. YOLOv8n was trained using the Ultralytics framework, whereas RF-DETR Nano was trained using its native implementation to preserve detector-specific training behavior. Performance was evaluated using mAP@50, mAP@75, mAP@50:95, precision, recall, and F1-score, with an additional CPU inference measurement under batch size 1. On the held-out test set, YOLOv8n achieved 88.66% mAP@50, 64.63% mAP@75, 58.94% mAP@50:95, and 88.23% macro F1, while RF-DETR Nano obtained 87.64%, 63.71%, 58.47%, and 89.72%, respectively. YOLOv8n retained a slight localization and precision advantage, whereas RF-DETR Nano achieved higher macro recall and macro F1. RF-DETR Nano also recorded lower desktop CPU inference time and memory use under the retained framework-specific benchmark. The results therefore indicate complementary detector behavior rather than uniform superiority of either model and are interpreted as a single-run, implementation-level comparison on the fixed export.

(Keywords)— sugarcane leaf disease, YOLOv8n, RF-DETR Nano, object detection, deep learning, precision agriculture

I. INTRODUCTION Sugarcane remains a strategically important crop in the Philippines because of its role in agricultural production, rural employment, and sugar-based value chains [1]. However, sugarcane productivity is repeatedly constrained by foliar and systemic diseases that degrade leaf health, reduce stalk quality, and lower sugar recovery. Brown rust, leaf scald, red rot, and smut are among the disease conditions that require timely recognition because delayed diagnosis can complicate field management and increase economic losses [2], [3]. Conventional disease assessment is still largely dependent on visual inspection by growers, technicians, and plant pathologists. Although expert judgment remains essential, manual inspection is labor-intensive, observer-dependent, and difficult to scale across large plantations. Early symptoms may also resemble abiotic stress, physical damage, or non-specific discoloration, which makes reliable lesion localization challenging under routine field monitoring [4]-[6]. These constraints motivate computer vision systems that can assist disease screening by providing faster and more consistent localization of symptomatic regions. Deep learning has improved plant disease recognition through image classification, object detection, and lesion localization methods [4], [5]. In sugarcane research, previous studies have investigated disease recognition, rust differentiation, and UAV-based disease monitoring using deep learning and image processing techniques [3], [6], [7], [12]. Rosal et al. further established a sugarcane-specific object detection benchmark by applying Faster R-CNN Inception V2 to an expert-annotated Philippine dataset [8]. That work demonstrated the feasibility of multiclass lesion detection but also reported performance degradation for visually similar disease categories, particularly red rot and brown rust. For deployment-oriented agricultural vision, detector selection is an engineering decision rather than a purely algorithmic choice. One-stage detectors such as YOLO are widely used because they provide a strong speed-accuracy trade-off and are accessible through mature software ecosystems [9], [10]. Compact transformer-based detectors such as RF-DETR aim to improve representation quality while retaining real-time detection capability [11]. However, direct evidence on how these lightweight detector families behave on the same Philippine sugarcane disease domain remains limited. This study addresses that gap by benchmarking YOLOv8n and RF-DETR Nano on a common model-ready export derived from the expert-annotated sugarcane dataset introduced by Rosal et al. [8]. The specific objectives are to: 1) train both detectors on the same disease domain using a shared prepared export; 2) evaluate validation and held-out test performance using mAP, precision, recall, and F1-score; 3) examine class-wise retrieval behavior for visually overlapping diseases; and 4) characterize the accuracy-latency trade-off using CPU inference measurements. The contribution is therefore positioned as a defensible benchmark and failure-behavior analysis rather than as a claim of universal architectural superiority.

II. METHODOLOGY The study used a quantitative experimental design to compare YOLOv8n and RF-DETR Nano for multiclass sugarcane leaf disease detection. The workflow consisted of dataset provenance review, preparation of a fixed model-ready export, detector training under implementation-native lightweight settings, validation-based assessment, held-out test evaluation, and CPU inference measurement. Fig. 2 summarizes the experimental workflow.

A. Dataset Provenance and Prepared Export The experiments build on the expert-annotated sugarcane leaf disease dataset introduced by Rosal et al. [8]. The source collection contained 800 high-resolution leaf images representing four disease classes: brown rust, leaf scald, red rot, and smut. The original images were acquired in Hagonoy, Davao del Sur, Philippines, under controlled capture conditions and were verified by certified plant pathologists [8]. For the present benchmark, the prepared export contained 2,720 training images, 173 validation images, and 171 held-out test images. The training partition contained 5,506 annotated disease instances, comprising 1,272 brown-rust, 1,384 leaf-scald, 1,636 red-rot, and 1,214 smut instances. The validation partition contained 382 instances distributed as 78 brown rust, 124 leaf scald, 97 red rot, and 83 smut, whereas the held-out test partition contained 311 instances comprising 38 brown rust, 85 leaf scald, 95 red rot, and 93 smut. The export also contained 95 training, 6 validation, and 3 test images with no annotated disease instances. Data augmentation was applied to the training partition, while the validation and test partitions were retained without augmentation. Although no exact byte-identical images were identified across the retained partitions, a complete per-image raw-to-export lineage map was not preserved; therefore, complete source-image independence cannot be retrospectively verified from the available records. Both detectors were trained and evaluated using the same fixed prepared export.

B. Preprocessing and Augmentation The export records describe auto-orientation and stretch resizing to 640 × 640 pixels. Training augmentation generated up to five versions per source and included horizontal flipping with probability 0.50; optional clockwise or counterclockwise 90° rotation; random cropping from 0% to 20%; free rotation from −15° to +15°; horizontal and vertical shear within ±10°; brightness and exposure adjustments within ±10%; Gaussian blur from 0 to 0.5 px; and salt-and-pepper noise affecting 0.16% of pixels. Validation and test exports were unaugmented. These records identify the transformations applied to each export partition but do not establish that related source images were grouped before splitting. The transformations were intended to increase geometric and photometric variation while retaining disease-related visual features. Because the source images were acquired under controlled conditions [8], performance on this augmented export does not establish robustness to field lighting, occlusion, background clutter, or symptom variation. Source-image independence and external validity therefore remain separate requirements for subsequent evaluation.

C. Detector Training Configuration YOLOv8n was implemented using the Ultralytics framework and initialized from the pretrained yolov8n.pt checkpoint [10]. Training was conducted for 300 epochs with an input size of 640 × 640 pixels, batch size of 34, and two dataloader workers. Automatic mixed precision was enabled on CUDA device 0. The retained training configuration recorded an initial learning-rate parameter of (lr 0=0.01), a final learning-rate factor of (lrf=0.01), and the Ultralytics auto optimizer setting, which allowed the framework to select the optimization configuration. Training used seed 0 with deterministic execution enabled. Checkpoint-based validation was performed throughout training, and runs/detect/train/weights/best.pt was retained as the checkpoint used for subsequent evaluation. RF-DETR Nano was implemented using the native rf-detr framework [11] and initialized from the pretrained rf-detr-nano.pth weights. Its detection head was reinitialized from the original 90 output classes to the four sugarcane disease classes considered in this study. Training was conducted for 200 epochs with a batch size of 8 and gradient accumulation over two steps, corresponding to an effective batch size of 16. The learning rate was 1 × 10 ⁻⁴ , and training used bfloat16 automatic mixed precision with CUDA acceleration. Although the prepared dataset images were exported at 640 × 640 pixels, the retained RF-DETR training log reported a native internal square multi-scale setting of 544 pixels. The optimizer was not explicitly specified in the retained training call and therefore followed the framework default; the worker count and random seed for this run were also not preserved. The checkpoint runs/rfdetr nano/checkpoint best ema.pth was retained for subsequent evaluation. The recorded experimental environment used Python 3.10.18, Ultralytics 8.4.14, PyTorch 2.11.0.dev20260214+cu128, and CUDA 12.8 on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 GPU with approximately 8 GB of device memory. The host system used Windows 10, an AMD Ryzen 9 7900X 12-Core Processor, and 31.14 GB of system memory. Complete training-time version information for several auxiliary packages was not retained. The comparison was not designed as an equal-compute ablation using identical optimizers, schedulers, input handling, or training budgets. Instead, each detector was trained using its implementation-native configuration preserved in the experiment records. Consequently, the measured differences reflect the combined effects of model architecture, implementation, optimization settings, training budget, and framework defaults. The observed performance gap should therefore be interpreted as benchmark-level behavior under the tested configurations rather than as evidence of architectural superiority in isolation.

D. Evaluation Metrics Both selected checkpoints were re-evaluated on the same 173 validation images and 171 test images using pycocotools.COCOeval for bounding-box average precision (AP). The COCO annotations were checked against the YOLO export by filename, per-image class counts, and box coordinates within a one-pixel rounding tolerance. Predictions were exported in COCO XYWH pixel coordinates with a native confidence floor of 0.01. The evaluator used IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in increments of 0.05, area = all, and maxDets = [1, 10, 100]. The reported metrics are mAP@50, mAP@75, and mAP@50:95. YOLOv8n used native class-aware non-maximum suppression (NMS) at IoU 0.70, max det = 300, and 640-pixel inference resolution. RF-DETR Nano used its native NMS-free postprocessor at 384-pixel resolution with 300 object queries. Neither detector used test-time augmentation. Disease channels 0–3 mapped to COCO categories 1–4; the additional unassigned RF-DETR output channel and unannotated COCO category 0 were excluded. Finite, positive-area RF-DETR boxes extending beyond image boundaries were retained without clipping. Class-wise precision, recall, and F1-score used confidence ≥ 0.50 and greedy one-to-one same-class matching at IoU ≥ 0.50. A prediction matched the highest-IoU unmatched ground-truth box; unmatched predictions counted as false positives and unmatched ground-truth boxes as false negatives. Macro precision, recall, and F1 were the arithmetic means of the four corresponding class-wise metrics; macro F1 was not computed from macro precision and recall. The unified evaluation used Python 3.10.18, PyTorch 2.11.0.dev20260214+cu128, CUDA 12.8, Ultralytics 8.4.36, RF-DETR 1.6.4, and pycocotools 2.0.11. These later evaluation versions are distinguished from the incompletely preserved training environment.

E. Reproducibility and Interpretation Boundaries Both detectors were evaluated using the same fixed prepared export, disease classes, validation partition, and held-out test partition. The retained export records indicate that augmentation was applied to the training partition while validation and test exports were unaugmented. No exact byte-identical files were identified across partitions; however, a complete raw-to-export lineage map was not retained, and related source-name overlap remains unresolved. Complete source-image independence therefore cannot be retrospectively established from the available records. In addition, the study used a single run per detector and implementation-native rather than matched-compute training configurations. The results are consequently interpreted as fixed-export benchmark behavior rather than as leakage-audited source-level generalization or architecture-only superiority.

Differences in the prepared export, partitioning, and evaluation pipeline preclude a direct comparison with the present results. The current test mAP@50 values of 88.66% for YOLOv8n and 87.64% for RF-DETR Nano should therefore be interpreted within this study’s fixed-export evaluation, rather than as verified improvements over the earlier detector. Several limitations constrain interpretation. Controlled source-image acquisition does not capture the full range of field lighting, occlusion, background clutter, leaf orientation, and symptom progression. Repeated source-name stems across partitions raise a risk of related-image overlap, and the missing raw-to-export manifest prevents verification of source-level independence. The validation and test exports are therefore not external field-validation sets. Unequal training configurations and a single run per detector prevent isolation of architectural effects and estimation of run-to-run variability. Historical software traceability is incomplete, and the CPU measurements lack common timing boundaries and repeated-run statistics. These limitations restrict both generalization and deployment claims.

IV. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK This study compared YOLOv8n and RF-DETR Nano on a fixed Philippine sugarcane disease export using a common detection evaluator. YOLOv8n achieved test mAP@50, mAP@75, and mAP@50:95 of 88.66%, 64.63%, and 58.94%, respectively, compared with 87.64%, 63.71%, and 58.47% for RF-DETR Nano. RF-DETR Nano achieved higher test macro recall and macro F1 (88.67% and 89.72%) than YOLOv8n (84.26% and 88.23%) and led on validation mAP. The findings indicate complementary performance under the tested configurations. Framework-specific CPU observations favor RF-DETR Nano numerically but do not establish a controlled runtime advantage. The single-run design and unverified source-image independence limit the strength of the conclusions. Future work should establish a raw-image identifier and source-to-export manifest, verify related-image groups, and use group-aware partitioning before training augmentation. If cross-partition derivatives are confirmed, both detectors should be retrained and evaluated on corrected partitions. Repeated-seed experiments, external field-acquired testing, and threshold-sensitivity analysis are needed to assess stability and generalization. Runtime evaluation should use common timing boundaries, warm-up procedures, thread settings, repeated measurements, and peak-memory monitoring before extending the comparison to low-power edge hardware.
